# Berk–Nash equilibrium: how feedback can sustain a mistaken view

A learner searches its existing models for the explanation that best fits experience. Its actions, in turn, determine which experiences it collects. Esponda and Pouzo's Berk–Nash equilibrium connects these two steps: beliefs best fit the feedback received, and actions are optimal under those beliefs. The two can support each other while remaining at odds with the real world.

## A two-action example

Consider an illustrative example. A learner chooses A or B, receiving 1 for success and 0 for failure. The true success probabilities are 0.6 for A and 0.8 for B, so B is better.

The learner considers only models in which A succeeds with probability θ and B with probability θ/2, where θ can be 0.4, 0.6, or 0.8. Every model ranks B below A. None can represent the true world.

If the learner always chooses A, long-run feedback supports θ = 0.6. This parameter correctly predicts A but assigns B a success probability of 0.3. The learner therefore keeps choosing A, and those choices keep producing evidence for θ = 0.6.

Trying B would expose the mistake. Yet as long as the model requires B's success probability to be half of A's, no parameter can explain both actions correctly. Exploration changes the evidence; changing the model class changes the available explanations.

## The best fit to experience

Start with one learner, finitely many actions and outcomes, and a fixed payoff function. Let a denote an action, y the feedback, and u(a,y) the payoff. The world generates feedback according to Q_W(y∣a). The learner considers models Q_θ(y∣a), indexed by a parameter set Θ. The frequency of choosing action a is ρ(a).

Frequently chosen actions receive more weight when assessing fit. The weighted KL divergence K measures prediction error, and Θ*(ρ) collects the parameters that minimize it:

```math
K(\rho,\theta)=\sum_a\rho(a)\,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(Q_W(\cdot\mid a)\,\|\,Q_\theta(\cdot\mid a)\right),
\qquad
\Theta^*(\rho)=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}K(\rho,\theta).
```

KL measures the extra expected log loss from predicting with the subjective model rather than the true distribution. An action never chosen has zero weight, so the data do not test it directly. Structural assumptions can still constrain its prediction: in the example, A and B share the same parameter.

With a fixed model class, stable action frequencies, and the relevant regularity conditions, Bayesian updating concentrates posterior probability on the best-fitting models. Differences in long-run average log likelihood correspond to differences in weighted KL divergence.

## Best-fitting beliefs must also support the actions

The learner may believe several models are possible. Let b be its probability distribution over θ. Its prediction for feedback is the corresponding mixture:

```math
\bar Q_b(y\mid a)=\int_\Theta Q_\theta(y\mid a)\,b(d\theta).
```

A single-agent Berk–Nash equilibrium requires the strategy ρ and belief b to satisfy two conditions.

First, all belief probability falls on the models that best fit the data generated by the current actions:

```math
b\bigl(\Theta^*(\rho)\bigr)=1.
```

Second, every action actually used maximizes expected payoff under that mixture belief:

```math
\rho(a)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 a\in\arg\max_{a'}\sum_y\bar Q_b(y\mid a')\,u(a',y).
```

The first condition connects feedback to beliefs; the second connects beliefs back to actions. In the example, b concentrates on θ = 0.6, and A is optimal under that belief, so both conditions hold. The paper also allows multiple players and private signals, with each player's feedback distribution affected by the others' strategies.

## Self-confirmation, Nash equilibrium, and learning convergence

[Self-confirming equilibrium](self-confirming-equilibrium.md) allows mistakes off the observed path: beliefs correctly predict observed feedback but misjudge untried actions. Berk–Nash also allows errors on the observed path because the true distribution may lie outside the model class.

When the model is correctly specified, Berk–Nash connects to self-confirming equilibrium. Adding strong identification, so that best-fitting models agree on predictions for every action, recovers the corresponding Nash conclusion. This rules out models that agree on observed evidence but disagree about untried actions.

Equilibrium describes mutually supporting beliefs and actions; convergence asks whether learning reaches them. The paper fixes the subjective model class, uses Bayesian updating, and studies games with small payoff perturbations. Theorem 2 shows that strategies which stabilize under the relevant optimal policies must satisfy the Berk–Nash conditions of the perturbed game.

The converse needs additional conditions. The 2019 correction requires weak identification at the equilibrium, a belief supporting it, and a prior with a specified conditional distribution on the best-fitting set; policies may have vanishing optimization errors. [Endogenous misspecified learning](endogenous-misspecified-learning.md) further examines which equilibria can become long-run limits of action.
