# Endogenous misspecified learning: which self-consistent states can persist?

[Berk–Nash equilibrium](berk-nash.md) allows a belief to support the current action, but the learner continues updating that belief. Fudenberg, Lanzani, and Strack ask which actions can remain unchanged as learning continues.

## Equally good explanations can recommend different actions

Several models can have the same average prediction loss while making different predictions and recommending different actions. New observations change the learner's confidence in these models, potentially changing its actions as well.

Ordinary Berk–Nash equilibrium requires the existence of a belief that assigns probability only to best-fitting models and makes the current action optimal. For an action to remain stable through learning, we must also ask what happens if the learner comes to trust another equally good explanation.

## A stable action must withstand changes in belief

Let a* denote a candidate limiting action and Θ*(a*) the models that best fit the feedback generated by repeatedly choosing it. Let μ be a belief over those models, and let rθ(a) be the expected payoff of action a under model θ. The paper gives the following necessary condition:

```math
a^*\in\arg\max_a\int r_\theta(a)\,\mu(d\theta)
\quad\text{for every }\mu\text{ such that }\mu(\Theta^*(a^*))=1.
```

This is the *uniform Berk–Nash* condition: the original action remains optimal under every allocation of belief across the best-fitting models. Changes in their posterior weights therefore leave the action optimal.

The result concerns Bayesian decision problems with a fixed subjective model class and finite sets of actions and outcomes. If optimal learning behavior converges, its limit must satisfy this condition. The paper also gives conditions under which strict equilibria can arise as long-run outcomes from suitable initial beliefs; convergence of general trajectories remains a separate question.

## From current consistency to lasting stability

Equilibrium explains why an agent keeps its current action. This paper asks why it can keep that action after further learning, as confidence shifts among its best-fitting explanations.

For RSI, this provides a dynamic baseline with fixed learning mechanisms. Expanding the model class, changing observation tools, or modifying the update rule changes the available explanations or the learning process. Stability conditions must then be reconsidered.
